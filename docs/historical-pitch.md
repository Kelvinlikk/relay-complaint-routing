# Two-minute pitch transcript

A customer writes about a payment the bank never applied. It could be a card payment or a mortgage payment. Choosing the wrong desk means another handoff. We built a router that can ask a person instead of guessing.

In a live run of our text-only prototype, a made-up complaint about a lien after mortgage payoff received the decision Mortgages. A second one mentions a charge, a transfer and a missing payment. Its decision was Human review. The system recommends a queue; it never resolves the complaint.

We trained on public complaints from an official federal archive, removed repeated templates, and held out later periods. We compared a simple text model, a pretrained language model, company and state, and a combination. Product labels and company responses were excluded from the inputs.

On the first test the locked rule routed 54.5 percent of arrivals automatically. Of those, 4.0 percent went to the wrong product queue. That denominator matters: a small error rate is useful only if the router actually takes work off someone's desk.

Five years later, using exactly the same rule, misrouting was 12.0 percent at 31.2 percent coverage. A score that once justified routing is not a promise about today's inbox. One hundred and eight of the wrong routes went to credit reports. Keep those manual and test the card queue first.

At four minutes of review, thirty dollars an hour, and twenty-five dollars per misroute, the June test implies a loss of 3,143 dollars per ten thousand eligible arrivals. Those are assumed costs. Approve a shadow pilot, time the work, and automate only queues that meet the five percent limit on local cases.

Narration is synthesized. Both example complaints are authored for this demonstration.
